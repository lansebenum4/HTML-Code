Rugby Union

A sport built on physical courage, teamwork, and respect - played in over 120 countries.

A rugby scrum - one of the sport's most iconic images, requiring strength, technique, and coordination from all eight forwards. Image source: Wikipedia

What Is Rugby Union?

Rugby union is a contact sport played between two teams of fifteen players on a rectangular grass pitch. The objective is to score more points than the opposing team by carrying, passing, or kicking the ball over the opponent's try line to score a try, or by kicking the ball between the posts for a penalty goal or drop goal.

Unlike football, players in rugby can carry the ball with their hands and run with it. However, the ball can only be passed backwards or sideways - never forward. This rule creates the distinctive flowing, lateral movement that characterizes the game. A match consists of two 40-minute halves.

Rugby union is governed by World Rugby (formerly the International Rugby Board), which was founded in 1886 and today has over 120 member unions. The sport is particularly popular in England, Ireland, Scotland, Wales, France, South Africa, New Zealand, Australia, Argentina, Japan, and the Pacific Island nations.

How Points Are Scored

  • Try (5 points) - Grounding the ball over the opponent's try line
  • Conversion (2 points) - A kick at goal after scoring a try
  • Penalty goal (3 points) - A kick at goal awarded after an infringement
  • Drop goal (3 points) - Dropping the ball and kicking it as it bounces

A team that scores a try is then awarded a conversion attempt - a kick at goal from a position in line with where the try was scored. This encourages teams to score tries close to the posts rather than in the corners.

History of Rugby

The traditional story of rugby's origin involves a student named William Webb Ellis at Rugby School in Warwickshire, England. In 1823, according to legend, Ellis picked up the ball during a football match and ran with it, inventing a new form of the game in the process. Historians have been skeptical of this story, but Rugby School did develop a distinct handling game in the early 19th century, and the school's rules were codified in 1845.

The Rugby Football Union (RFU) was founded in 1871 by representatives of 21 clubs. The first international match was played the same year, between England and Scotland in Edinburgh. Scotland won by two tries to one. These early internationals established a competitive tradition that continues today in the annual Six Nations Championship.

Rugby spread rapidly through the British Empire during the late 19th and early 20th centuries. British settlers and soldiers took the game to South Africa, New Zealand, Australia, and the Pacific Islands. In many of these countries, particularly New Zealand and South Africa, rugby became a central part of national identity.

The Great Schism - Rugby Union vs Rugby League

In 1895, a dispute over payment of players caused a split in English rugby. Northern clubs, whose working-class players could not afford to miss wages for training and matches, wanted to pay players for lost time. The RFU refused. Twenty-one clubs broke away to form the Northern Rugby Football Union - which eventually became rugby league. The two codes developed differently: rugby league reduced the team to 13 players and simplified the rules, while union kept 15 and retained the complex ruck and maul laws. Union remained amateur until 1995, when it finally went professional.

Positions and the Set Piece

Rugby union divides its fifteen players into two groups: the eight forwards and the seven backs. The forwards are generally larger and more physical, responsible for winning possession at scrums, lineouts, rucks, and mauls. The backs are usually faster and more agile, responsible for moving the ball wide and creating scoring opportunities.

The Forwards

  • Loosehead prop (1) - Anchors the left side of the scrum
  • Hooker (2) - Hooks the ball in scrums, throws into lineouts
  • Tighthead prop (3) - Anchors the right side of the scrum
  • Locks (4 and 5) - Provide power in the scrum and win lineout ball
  • Flankers (6 and 7) - Mobile forwards who jackal for the ball and carry
  • Number 8 - Ball-carrying forward who links with the backs

The Backs

  • Scrum-half (9) - Feeds the scrum and distributes ball from rucks
  • Fly-half (10) - The key decision-maker, directs play and kicks
  • Inside centre (12) - Powerful carrier who runs direct lines
  • Outside centre (13) - Links the attack and defends the midfield
  • Wingers (11 and 14) - Finishers who score tries on the flanks
  • Fullback (15) - Last line of defence, covers kicks, counter-attacks

Major Competitions

The Rugby World Cup, first held in 1987, is the sport's premier international event. It is held every four years and is the third-most watched sporting event in the world after the FIFA World Cup and the Olympics. New Zealand's All Blacks are the most successful team in World Cup history, winning the tournament three times.

At club level, the sport's top competitions vary by country. In England, the Gallagher Premiership features 10 top clubs. France has the Top 14. In the southern hemisphere, teams from South Africa, New Zealand, and Australia compete in the United Rugby Championship and the Super Rugby competition.

Rugby and Culture

In New Zealand, rugby is more than a sport - it is a cornerstone of national identity. The All Blacks, New Zealand's national team, are the most successful side in the history of international rugby, winning approximately 77% of all Test matches played. Their pre-match haka - a traditional Maori war dance performed before every game - is one of the most recognizable rituals in world sport.

In South Africa, the national team's 1995 World Cup victory, just one year after the end of apartheid, became a powerful symbol of national reconciliation. The story was captured in the film Invictus, depicting President Nelson Mandela's support for the Springboks as a way of uniting the country. South Africa went on to win the World Cup again in 2007, 2019, and 2023, becoming the most successful team in tournament history.

In Wales, the national team's performances are an intensely felt matter of national pride. Welsh rugby has produced some of the game's greatest players, including Gareth Edwards, Barry John, and more recently Alun Wyn Jones, who became the world's most capped international rugby player with over 160 test appearances.
